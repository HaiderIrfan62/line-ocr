Your OCR model, one line at a time

How “A boy rides a swing.” becomes text

Follow one real validation image through your trained models: the CNN that turns pixels into columns, the BiLSTM that adds context, and the two heads that turn columns into characters. Every number and picture on this page comes from your checkpoints.

Step 1 · The input

An image is just a grid of numbers

The renderer drew the line, blurred it, added noise and scaled it to a height of 32 pixels. The model never sees letters, only 32 × 272 = 8,704 brightness values between 0 (black) and 1 (white). Here is the first capital “A” up close: each square is one pixel and its number.

Step 2 · CNN

Squeeze the height away, keep the width

Each convolution slides 3 × 3 filters over the image and lights up where it finds a pattern: edges first, then strokes, then parts of letters. Pooling halves the size. The trick is that the height is halved four times (32 → 2) and then averaged to 1, but the width only twice (272 → 68). The result is a row of 68 columns, and each column describes a 4-pixel-wide vertical strip of the image.

Step 3 · Columns become time steps

68 strips, read left to right

Because the width was divided by 4, column t of the CNN output corresponds to pixels 4t to 4t + 3 of the image. From here on, the line is a sequence of 68 steps, just like a sentence is a sequence of words.

Step 4 · BiLSTM

Every strip hears from its neighbours

A strip on its own may hold half a letter. The BiLSTM runs two LSTMs over the 68 steps: one left to right, one right to left, each carrying a memory as it goes. Their outputs are joined, so every step gets 256 + 256 = 512 numbers that describe the strip in the context of the whole line.

Step 5a · CTC head

One guess per strip, then collapse

The CTC head is a single linear layer: for each of the 68 steps it turns 512 numbers into 73 scores, one per token, and softmax turns them into probabilities. The heatmap shows the probabilities for the blank and for each character that appears in the line. Your trained model is very sure of itself: almost every column has one clear winner.

Step 5b · Attention decoder

Write a character, look, write the next

The attention decoder does not guess per strip. It writes one character at a time. Before each character it scores all 68 strips, turns the scores into weights that add up to 1, and reads a weighted average of the strips (the context vector). Then it predicts the next character from that context and what it has written so far.

Why the attention model fails from scratch

A chicken-and-egg problem

To learn good image features, the CNN needs the decoder to look at the right strip. To learn where to look, the decoder needs image features that are worth looking at. At the start neither is true, so the gradient that reaches the CNN is almost noise.

Meanwhile there is a shortcut. Teacher forcing always feeds the correct previous character, and captions are very repetitive, so the decoder can lower its loss just by learning English: after “A man in a” the next word is probably “blue” or “black”. That is exactly the sentence every failing run ended up writing.

CTC has no shortcut. Each step must predict the character at its own strip, so from the first batch the gradient tells every column of the CNN what it should see.

The fix is to break the loop: give the CNN a useful signal early (a joint CTC loss, or starting from the CTC encoder) or make the first alignments easy (short lines first). The scoring function and the learning rate were never the bottleneck.
